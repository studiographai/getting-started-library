# Plain Language Content Design

Principles for writing content that a busy, non-expert reader can scan and act on in seconds — adapted from the [18F Content Guide](https://github.com/18F/content-guide), a public-domain (CC0) style guide built for U.S. government digital services, where "the reader is in a hurry and the stakes are real" is the default assumption for every page.

## Write for scanning, not reading

Most people scan a page before they read any of it — they're deciding whether it's worth their time. Front-load the point: put the most important information in the first sentence of a paragraph and the first few words of a headline, because that's often all that gets read. Break content into short sections with descriptive headers (not clever ones) so a scanning reader can navigate by headers alone and still get the gist.

## Write for a task, not a topic

"About Our Services" describes a topic; "What We Can Build for You" describes what the reader is there to figure out. Before writing a section, name the question the reader actually has — "Is this for me?", "How much does it cost?", "What happens next?" — and answer that question directly rather than describing the subject in general terms.

## Use plain words over formal ones

Prefer the word people actually use in conversation: "use" over "utilize," "help" over "facilitate," "start" over "commence," "about" over "approximately." Plain words aren't less professional — they're faster to process, and speed of understanding is itself a form of respect for the reader's time. Reserve technical or specialized terms for audiences who need that precision, and define them on first use otherwise.

## Talk directly to the reader

Second person ("you," "your") is almost always clearer and warmer than third person or passive constructions. "You'll get a confirmation email" is more direct than "A confirmation email will be sent." This single habit does more to make institutional writing feel human than any amount of tone-polishing after the fact.

## Keep sentences and paragraphs short

One idea per paragraph, one main clause per sentence where possible. If a sentence needs three commas to hold together, it's probably two sentences trying to share one. Short paragraphs aren't a stylistic preference on the web — they're what makes a page scannable on a phone screen, which is how most people will actually encounter it.

## Applying this in Studiograph

For landing pages and publication covers, write the headline as if it were the only sentence the reader would see — because for a large fraction of readers, it is. For longer publications, open each major section with a one-sentence answer to the question that section exists to address, then support it — don't make the reader wait for the payoff at the end of the paragraph.
