# Clear, Concise Writing (Elements of Style)

A small set of rules for economical, unambiguous prose, drawn from William Strunk Jr.'s 1918 *The Elements of Style* — a public-domain text still in wide practical use over a century later (referenced here via its [agent-packaged form](https://github.com/obra/the-elements-of-style)). These rules are about cutting friction between the reader and the point, not about tone or personality — pair with a voice-specific skill for that.

## Omit needless words

The single most useful edit is deletion. "The reason why he left is because he was needed at home" becomes "He left because he was needed at home." Every sentence should contain no unnecessary words, every paragraph no unnecessary sentences — not because short is a virtue in itself, but because every extra word is a small tax on the reader's attention. After drafting, reread specifically hunting for words that could be cut without losing meaning: "in order to" → "to," "due to the fact that" → "because," "at this point in time" → "now."

## Prefer the active voice

"The infographic was designed by the team" buries the actor and adds words for no gain. "The team designed the infographic" is shorter and clearer about who did what. Active voice isn't a hard rule — passive voice is correct when the actor genuinely doesn't matter ("the report was published in 2024") — but active should be the default you deviate from on purpose, not the one you fall into by accident.

## Use definite, concrete, specific language

Vague generalities read as filler even when they're technically true. "The results were significant" tells the reader nothing; "revenue grew 34% year over year" tells them everything. Whenever a sentence could apply to almost anything, it's a sign it needs a real number, name, or example in place of the abstraction.

## Keep related words together

The position of words in a sentence signals their relationship — modifiers should sit next to what they modify. "She only sent the draft to one client" and "She sent the draft to only one client" say different things; sloppy placement of "only," "just," "even," and similar qualifiers is a common source of accidental ambiguity. Read a revised sentence back literally to check the words landed where you meant them to.

## Put the emphatic word at the end

The end of a sentence is the position of greatest emphasis — whatever matters most should land there, not get buried in the middle. "We missed the deadline, unfortunately" is weaker than "Unfortunately, we missed the deadline," which is weaker still than restructuring to end on the consequence that actually matters to the reader.

## Applying this in Studiograph

Run this pass after content is drafted, not while drafting — trying to write economically and generate ideas at the same time slows both down. On a headline or CTA, apply "omit needless words" first; it usually does more work than any other single edit. On body copy for a report or publication, check the active-voice and concrete-language rules paragraph by paragraph, since these are the two habits that erode fastest under deadline pressure.
